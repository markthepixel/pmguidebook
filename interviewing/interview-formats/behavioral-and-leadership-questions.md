# Behavioral and Leadership Questions

Behavioral and leadership questions ask you to explain how you've handled real situations. An interviewer may ask about a disagreement, an uncertain decision, a failure, or a time you influenced a team without direct authority. Choose an experience that answers the question and explain what you personally did, why you did it, and what happened. The communication principles in [The Art of Storytelling](../../people-skills/communication/the-art-of-storytelling.md) can help a listener follow the situation, but the account still needs to reflect your actual contribution and the limits of what you know.

## Example

Imagine you're discussing a disagreement from a project where you worked on a reporting tool. A sales lead wanted a custom dashboard for a prospective customer. Engineering was already committed to repairing reports that current customers couldn't reliably use. Your responsibility was to recommend which work the team should prioritize; you didn't control the sales commitment or the engineering schedule by yourself.

In explaining the situation, you describe how you asked the sales lead what the customer needed to decide and why the existing reports were insufficient. You also worked with engineering to understand the repair effort. You considered delaying the repair, building the full dashboard, or preparing a limited report using existing functionality. You recommended the limited report because it could help the customer evaluate the product without postponing the reliability work. You explain the cost of that choice: the sales team would have to set expectations about what was and wasn't available, and the limited report might not meet the customer's needs.

The team agreed to try the limited report, but the customer still required features the product didn't support and didn't proceed with the purchase. You describe how this led you to check a prospective customer's essential requirements earlier, before recommending a demonstration. You also explain why protecting the reliability work for existing customers was still a reasonable choice, even though the limited report didn't lead to a sale.

## Pain Points

Using “we” throughout an answer can leave the interviewer unsure of your contribution, while taking personal credit for team results misrepresents the work. Too much background can also crowd out the decision the question asks about. You may not have a precise outcome measure, especially for a recent project; explain the evidence you do have and what remains unknown. A failure can be relevant when you examine your own judgment and learning rather than assigning all responsibility to other people.

## Practical Exercise

Choose a real disagreement from work, study, or a team project. Using [Cross-Functional Leadership](../../people-skills/leadership/cross-functional-leadership.md) as a reference, explain your responsibility, the choice you made, and what happened. What would you change about your approach now?
