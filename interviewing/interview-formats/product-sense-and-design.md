# Product Sense and Design

Product sense and design questions explore how you understand a user's needs and choose a product response. You may be asked to improve an existing product or design something for a particular audience. Start by clarifying the goal and any important constraints, then explain which users and problems you would focus on. The research covered in [Customer Discovery and Research](../../process-skills/discovery/customer-discovery-and-research.md) provides a foundation for this reasoning. In an interview, distinguish what you know from what you would need to investigate, and explain the assumptions behind your recommendation.

## Example

Imagine an interviewer asks you to improve a public library app. You clarify that the goal is to help more library members borrow books, rather than increase time spent in the app. You consider several users, including people searching for a particular title and people browsing for something new. For the discussion, you choose members looking for a specific book because the path from searching to borrowing gives you a focused task to examine. You explain that this choice is an assumption you would check with the library's data and member research.

You describe a possible problem: a member finds a title but cannot tell which branch has an available copy or how to reserve it. You compare making availability clearer with adding personalized recommendations. Recommendations could help someone choose a book, but they wouldn't resolve the problem you've selected. You propose showing branch availability and the next step for placing a hold alongside the search result. You also consider a member who cannot travel to the branch with an available copy, which raises a question about transfers or other formats the library supports.

The interviewer then says that availability information is sometimes out of date. You adjust your recommendation to show when it was updated and investigate how a hold is confirmed before promising a book is ready. You would test whether members can find and reserve a suitable copy, while watching for failed reservations. This changes the proposal from a screen improvement alone to a decision that depends on reliable information from the library's systems.

## Pain Points

Jumping to features can leave the user problem unexplained. A long list of user groups can create the same problem if you never choose whose needs to address first. Frameworks may help you organize a discussion, but filling every category doesn't justify a decision. Avoid presenting invented research as fact, and be willing to change your recommendation when a new constraint undermines it. [Prioritization](../../process-skills/strategy/prioritization.md) can help you compare alternatives without treating a score as the answer.

## Practical Exercise

Choose a product and one user problem you would address. Compare two possible changes and explain why you would choose one. Ask a practice partner to add a constraint, or choose one yourself. How does it affect your recommendation?
