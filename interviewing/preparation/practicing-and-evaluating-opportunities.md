# Practicing and Evaluating Opportunities

Interview practice gives you a way to find gaps in your reasoning and communication before an actual discussion. A useful practice session includes answering aloud, responding to follow-up questions, and reviewing specific feedback. The goal is to explain your thinking clearly while remaining able to reconsider it. As you prepare, also consider what you need from the role and what questions would help you understand how the team actually works.

## Example

Imagine you're practicing a question about improving a grocery delivery app with a colleague. You agree to use the same time limit as the upcoming interview and ask your colleague to interrupt with questions. You spend most of the session describing features. When they ask which customer problem you're addressing, you realize that you haven't explained why a customer would need any of the changes.

Afterward, you ask your colleague to point to where your reasoning became difficult to follow. They note that you named several user groups without choosing one, so your recommendation kept changing. For the next session, you practice explaining which group you would investigate first and why. You use a different product and ask your colleague to challenge that choice. This tests whether you can apply the feedback beyond a single rehearsed answer. The questions in [Giving and Receiving Feedback](../../people-skills/leadership/giving-and-receiving-feedback.md) can also help you discuss what needs improvement.

When you speak with the hiring team, you use the discussion to learn about the opportunity. If access to customers matters to you, you ask how a recent product decision used customer research and who conducted it. You compare that example with the responsibilities described earlier. If different interviewers give conflicting accounts of who makes product decisions, you ask for clarification before drawing a conclusion. You leave with notes about what fits your needs and what remains uncertain, rather than judging the role only by how well you felt you performed.

## Pain Points

Memorizing a polished answer can make it difficult to respond when the interviewer changes a constraint. Feedback such as “be more strategic” is also hard to act on without a specific moment or missing decision to examine. Practice partners may have preferences that differ from the hiring team, so consider why a suggestion would improve your reasoning before adopting it. Follow the company's stated rules for AI and other tools, and clarify an unclear take-home scope instead of assuming that more work is always expected.

## Practical Exercise

Record yourself answering an interview question, or practice with a partner. Find one point where your reasoning is difficult to follow and try the answer again with a specific change. What changed, and what still needs work?
