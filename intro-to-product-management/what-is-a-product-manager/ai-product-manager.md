# AI Product Manager

An AI Product Manager can mean someone responsible for a product that uses artificial intelligence. However, AI also changes how a Product Manager can do their own work, whether or not their product includes an AI feature. A PM might use AI to explore data, create a working prototype, or test different approaches to a user problem. The PM still needs to understand the problem and evaluate whether the work is useful and correct.

My outlook is that AI puts increasing pressure on PM roles centered mainly on passing information between people. I see Product Managers contributing more as leaders who choose worthwhile problems and guide decisions, or as builders who turn ideas into something they can test. These contributions can overlap within the same role. Leadership includes aligning people, making tradeoffs, and taking responsibility for outcomes, while building can involve prototyping and investigating data with AI. This doesn't mean every PM needs to become an engineer. Customer discovery, domain expertise, and coordination that resolves real disagreements remain valuable parts of product management.

## Example

Imagine you're a Product Manager at a company that provides scheduling software for small businesses. Customers have difficulty finding instructions for changing an appointment, and the team is considering an AI assistant. Before choosing a solution, you review support requests and speak with customers to understand where they get stuck. You find that the immediate problem is finding the right instructions, so you propose testing an assistant that answers questions using the company's help articles. Changing appointments on a customer's behalf is outside the initial scope.

To explore the idea, you use an approved AI tool to build a prototype with public help articles and fictional customer questions. You don't upload private support conversations or customer records. You work with a support specialist to prepare questions with expected answers, including questions the articles cannot answer. When the prototype confidently describes a cancellation policy that doesn't exist, you record the failure and change the design so that answers point to the relevant article and unsupported questions can be passed to support. You then test again, including whether the linked article actually supports each answer.

The prototype gives the team something concrete to discuss, but it doesn't establish that the assistant is ready for customers. You work with engineers to investigate access controls, response time, and what happens when the AI service is unavailable. You also compare the cost of answering a question with the value of helping a customer complete their task. A simpler improvement to help-center search may be sufficient, especially if the assistant remains unreliable on common questions.

In this situation, choosing the problem, agreeing on scope, and deciding what evidence would justify further investment are leadership contributions. Making and testing the prototype are builder contributions. The same PM can do both while relying on support, design, and engineering expertise. The discussions described in [Working with Engineers](../../process-skills/development/working-with-engineers.md) still matter when a PM can produce a working demonstration themselves.

## Pain Points

AI-generated work can appear convincing even when it contains errors, and a prototype may hide problems that only appear with real users or sensitive data. A PM can also spend time building an impressive solution before establishing that it addresses a worthwhile problem. Evaluating the output, understanding the limits of permitted data use, and involving the right specialists remain part of the work. Moving faster doesn't remove responsibility for the decisions being made.

## Practical Exercise

Choose a small AI-assisted prototype for a product problem you're familiar with. Sketch how you would test whether it helps the user, including an expected result and a case it should pass to a person. If you build it, use only fictional or public data and tools you're permitted to use.
