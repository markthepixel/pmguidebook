# Solution Design and Validation

Solution design and validation involves developing and testing potential solutions to the problems identified through your research. This process can include creating prototypes, conducting usability tests, and gathering feedback from users. Solution design and validation is crucial for ensuring that your product effectively solves user problems and delivers value. It is also sometimes called Hypothesis testing.

## Example

Continuing with the Tinder example, after identifying the problem and conducting customer discovery and research, the Product Manager and their team have decided to test two new features. The first feature is an improved matching algorithm that takes into account shared interests, and the second is an enhanced chat feature that is more user-friendly.

Before building both features, the Product Manager identifies what they most need to learn. For the chat feature, they could create a small working conversation flow and ask potential users to start a conversation and respond to a message. Sample profiles and simulated matches are sufficient for that task, as long as the PM makes those limits clear. They observe where users struggle and change the flow before committing to more development. This test can reveal interaction problems, but it cannot show whether a new matching algorithm improves real matches.

To test behavior in the product, the Product Manager decides to create a minimum viable product (MVP). The MVP is a simplified version of the feature, providing just enough functionality to test the concept with users. The Product Manager collaborates with the design and engineering teams to develop it, ensuring it aligns with the app's existing design and functionality. They scope each feature around the assumption being tested rather than adding every proposed chat enhancement.

Once the MVP is ready, the Product Manager decides to conduct an A/B test to evaluate its effectiveness. With the analyst and engineers, they define the comparison, success measures, and safeguards, then choose a sample size and duration appropriate to the change they need to detect. A small percentage of a large user base is not, by itself, a reason that a test will produce a useful answer.

The Product Manager and their team monitor the usage of the new features over the agreed testing period. They track metrics such as how many matches are made using the improved algorithm, how many messages are sent using the enhanced chat feature, and whether there was any change in user satisfaction and engagement.

Suppose the test shows a 20% increase in match satisfaction and 30% more messages for the respective features. The Product Manager and data analyst check the comparisons, uncertainty, and any negative effects before interpreting the result. More messages might mean better conversations, or more effort to achieve the same outcome. Feedback from users helps them understand which explanation fits.

If the evidence supports continuing, the Product Manager decides to roll out the features to a larger group of users for further testing. They agree on the next release group and continue monitoring whether the benefits hold and whether new problems appear.

The Product Manager presents the findings and remaining risks to the leadership team and proposes the next step. If a full roll-out is approved, the team plans the remaining implementation and release work.

Throughout this process, the Product Manager continues to gather feedback from users and makes adjustments to the features based on this feedback. This iterative process of design, testing, and refinement helps to ensure that the final features are well-received by users and achieve the desired impact of improving user satisfaction and engagement.

## Pain Points

Solution design and validation can be challenging due to the complexity of designing effective solutions and the need for iterative testing and refinement. It requires a deep understanding of user needs, strong collaboration with design and engineering teams, and the ability to interpret and apply user feedback effectively. Additionally, the results of a test can change dramatically with the size of the sample group.

## Practical Exercise

Think about a product you really enjoy using. Choose one assumption behind a proposed improvement. What is the smallest sketch, working flow, or experiment that could test it? Describe a task for a real user, what you would observe, and what the result would not establish.
